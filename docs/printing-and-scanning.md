# Printing and scanning

A barcode that looks fine on screen can fail at the counter. Work through these in
order; each one has cost us a reprint at some point.

## 1. Print at 100%

Every browser's print dialog offers a scale control. "Fit to page" and "Shrink to
fit" both change the module width, which is the one dimension the scanner actually
reads. Set it to 100%, paper to US Letter, margins to none.

## 2. Use the right paper type

Laser printers run hotter than inkjets and can curl label stock through the fuser.
Check the stock's printer-type rating before feeding a full sheet; one wrinkled
sheet ruins the run.

## 3. Test on plain paper first

Print a page of labels onto ordinary paper, then hold it up against the real sheet
backlit. If the rows line up, the stock and the page settings agree. Look at the
**bottom** row, not the top — pitch errors accumulate downward and the last row is
where they show.

## 4. Scan the actual label

Scan the printed label with the scanner you will actually use, not a phone app.
Phone scanners tolerate lower contrast and lighter ink than laser-based retail
scanners do.

## 5. Quiet zones

The white space either side of the bars is part of the symbol. A label design that
puts a graphic or the product photo right up against the bars will fail even
though every bar is in the right place. Keep at least 9 modules of white space
(order of 0.12 in) on both sides.

## 6. Check digit versus licence

The check digit proves the arithmetic of the twelve digits is self-consistent. It
says nothing about ownership, and it does not make the number a GTIN that a
retailer will accept. Invented numbers are fine for internal shelf labels and
stock rooms; selling through retail or listing on a marketplace needs a GTIN
assigned to your business, normally from GS1.

## 7. Ink and contrast

Thermal transfer and laser both print adequate contrast on matte stock. Direct
thermal fades with heat and time, which matters if labels sit in a warehouse or a
vehicle.
